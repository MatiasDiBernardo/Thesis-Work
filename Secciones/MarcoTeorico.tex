\subsection{AUDIO QUALITY DESCRIPTORS}
\subsubsection{Signal degradation metrics}

Signal degradation metrics objectively quantify the difference between a reference signal (usually the clean or high-quality signal) and a processed or degraded signal (for example, a compressed signal or one with background noise). The most widely used metrics in speech and TTS evaluation are described below.

The Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality (PESQ) metric is an objective index designed to predict the perceived quality of speech in telephony and communication systems (\cite{ITU-T_P.862}). It compares the reference signal and the degraded signal by means of a perceptual model that includes stages of time alignment, perceptual modeling, and mapping to an MOS-LQO scale (Mean Opinion Score — Listening Quality Objective). PESQ was originally standardized as ITU-T Recommendation P.862 and is widely used for the evaluation of codecs and telephone transmissions.

This metric is limited by the frequency range it evaluates (from 200 Hz to 3500 Hz). For a more comprehensive analysis, the metric known as POLQA emerged, which is the third generation of ITU metrics for general speech quality assessment, ITU-T P.863 (\cite{ITU-T_P.863_2018}). It improves on and extends PESQ in bandwidth (including super-wideband and fullband), is more robust against certain modern distortions (equalization, delays, wideband coding), and provides more reliable MOS predictions in current scenarios.

To objectively evaluate the quality of the audio signal, there are metrics such as the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) and the Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SI-SDR). The SNR is the ratio between the power of the useful signal and the power of the background noise, usually expressed in decibels (Equation \ref{eq:snr}). It is a simple physical measure, very useful for characterizing capture or transmission conditions, but its correlation with human perception can be poor when distortions are non-Gaussian or non-additive, or when there are phase and time alterations; for this reason, it is usually used together with perceptual metrics.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:snr}
    \text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10} \left( \frac{P_{\text{signal}}}{P_{\text{noise}}} \right)
\end{equation}

SI-SDR is a modified and more robust version of the traditional SDR, designed to avoid penalties due to amplitude scaling between the reference and the estimate (\cite{SISDR}). In practice, SI-SDR projects the estimated signal onto the reference (removing the scale difference) and computes the resulting signal-to-residual ratio; owing to its formulation, it is widely used in source separation and in the evaluation of time-domain denoising or separation networks.

Finally, among speech signal quality metrics one can include the Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI), which estimates speech intelligibility under noisy conditions by means of a correlation measure between time-frequency segments of the clean signal and the degraded signal (\cite{STOI_ref}). It was proposed to predict the intelligibility of processed signals (e.g. spectral filtering, separation) and has shown high correlation with human intelligibility experiments in many scenarios. It does not measure naturalness, but intelligibility.

Owing to their mathematical definition, the computation of these metrics is intrusive, which means that they strictly require a clean reference signal (or the isolated noise signal, in the case of SNR) to quantify degradation through direct comparison. In empirical application contexts, such as the curation of \textit{in-the-wild} datasets, this original unaltered signal is rarely available.

To circumvent this practical limitation, blind or non-intrusive estimation methods have emerged, which make it possible to predict the quality of an audio recording by analyzing only the degraded signal. Whereas traditional blind estimators were based on statistical modeling of the characteristics of the vocal tract and of the noise, the current state of the art employs deep learning architectures (convolutional networks, transformers, and self-supervised representations). These neural systems are trained on databases that do have a reference in order to learn a direct mapping between the acoustic characteristics of the input audio and the scores that the intrusive metrics would theoretically yield. Recent and widely used examples in the literature include objective estimation frameworks such as \textit{Torchaudio-Squim} (\cite{squim}), designed to predict PESQ and SI-SDR values without a reference, as well as models aimed at predicting subjective perceptual quality (MOS), such as NISQA (\cite{nisqa}) and DNSMOS (\cite{dns_mos}).

It should be noted that no metric captures all perceptual aspects simultaneously (naturalness, intelligibility, artifacts, coloration). In the evaluation of TTS and speech processing systems, it is common to combine intrusive metrics (PESQ/POLQA, SI-SDR, MCD) with intelligibility measures (STOI) and, when possible, subjective tests (MOS).

\subsubsection{Environment metrics}

Environment metrics (or room acoustics metrics) describe properties of the room or of the sound field that affect the perception of speech and music.

One of the main parameters is the reverberation time T, classically defined as the time it takes for the sound level to decay by 60 dB after the source is switched off (Sabine). For practical reasons, estimators such as $T_{30}$ (slope of the decay between -5 dB and -35 dB extrapolated to -60 dB) or $T_{20}$ (between -5 and -25 dB) are computed. $T_{30}$ is commonly used in the objective characterization of rooms and is standardized by ISO 3382 (\cite{T30}). The values of T affect intelligibility, clarity, and the sense of acoustic spaciousness.

Other parameters used to evaluate audio perception in enclosures are clarity $C_{50}$ (for speech) and $C_{80}$ (for music), which measure the ratio between early and late energy. These parameters are defined mathematically as:

\begin{equation}
    C_{50} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\int_{0}^{50,\mathrm{ms}} p^{2}(t)dt}{\int_{50,\mathrm{ms}}^{\infty} p^{2}(t)dt}
\end{equation}

where $p(t)$ is the impulse response at the listening position. High values of $C_{50}$ indicate a greater proportion of early energy (better speech intelligibility).

$D_{50}$ (often expressed as a percentage) is the fraction of energy arriving in the first 50 ms relative to the total energy; it is another way of quantifying clarity/intelligibility for speech (related to $C_{50}$). It is computed from the impulse response and is relevant for the evaluation of educational spaces, conference rooms, and recording studios.

\subsubsection{Blind estimation of acoustic parameters}
The standardized definitions of $T_{30}$, $C_{50}$, and $D_{50}$ require measuring the room impulse response. In speech recordings obtained outside controlled conditions, this response is usually not available; blind estimation therefore seeks to infer equivalent parameters from the observed signal, without knowledge of the source or the room geometry. The first methods statistically modeled the reverberant tail to estimate the reverberation time from connected speech \cite{ratnam2003blind}.

Current methods learn this relationship from examples of speech and known impulse responses. For instance, it has been proposed to estimate $C_{50}$ from the spectrogram of monophonic speech using a CNN--LSTM network \cite{gamper2020c50}. These predictions allow \emph{in-the-wild} collections to be compared in a practical way, but they are estimates that depend on the training domain and do not replace a physical measurement of the room. In this thesis, a CNN-based estimator, validated for Argentine Spanish voices, is used to obtain $T_{30}$, $C_{50}$, and $D_{50}$ without requiring impulse response measurements \cite{Maxi2}.


\subsubsection{Speech metrics}

Speech metrics describe prosodic and spectral properties of the voice that influence naturalness and speaker identification. Within this category, one of the main metrics is the fundamental frequency of the voice $F_0$, whose value represents the main vibration frequency of the vocal folds of different speakers (\cite{F0_desc}) and constitutes the basis of \emph{pitch} perception. It is estimated by means of pitch detection algorithms (autocorrelation, cepstrum, algorithms based on probabilistic models) and is used for prosodic analysis, intonation control in TTS, and naturalness evaluation. The statistical distribution of F\textsubscript{0}, its temporal variation (contour), and its relationship with energy are indicators used in both objective and perceptual evaluation.

Another metric widely used in the analysis of TTS systems is the Mel-Cepstral Distortion (MCD). MCD (sometimes called Mel-Cepstral Distance) quantifies the distance between two sequences of mel-cepstral coefficients (for example, natural signal vs. synthesized signal) and is expressed in dB (\cite{mcd_metric}). The mathematical expression of this metric for a time frame $t$ is presented in Equation \ref{eq:mcd}:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mcd}
    \text{MCD} = \frac{10}{\ln 10} \sqrt{2 \sum_{k=1}^{K} (y_{t,k} - \hat{y}_{t,k})^2}
\end{equation}

where $y_{t,k}$ and $\hat{y}_{t,k}$ represent the $k$-th mel-cepstral coefficient of the reference signal and of the evaluated signal, respectively, and $K$ is the total number of coefficients considered. It is commonly computed as a square root of the normalized sum of squares between coefficient vectors and is widely used to evaluate spectral quality in TTS and vocoders; however, its correlation with perceived quality is not perfect and it must be complemented with perceptual tests.

\subsection{TEXT-TO-SPEECH (TTS)}
Text-to-speech (TTS) systems convert text into a speech signal (\cite{survey1}).
Historically, they can be grouped into three major approaches:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Concatenative approach: These systems assemble prerecorded speech fragments (units) to form utterances. They offer high naturalness when the corpus is homogeneous and extensive, but have low flexibility and a high collection cost (\cite{concatenative}).
    \item Parametric approach: These systems model acoustic parameters (for example, by means of hidden Markov models) and then synthesize the signal from the predicted parameters. They are more flexible and require a smaller corpus, although their perceptual quality is usually inferior to recorded speech (\cite{parametrico}).
    \item Neural approach: These systems employ neural networks to map text to intermediate representations (e.g.\ mel-spectrograms) and neural vocoders to generate the waveform. Within this group there are autoregressive variants (higher fidelity but slower) and non-autoregressive variants (faster and more scalable). The highest-quality current systems combine a spectrogram prediction model, such as Tacotron2 (\cite{tacotron}) or FastSpeech (\cite{fastspeech}), with a neural vocoder, such as WaveNet (\cite{wavenet}) or HiFi-GAN (\cite{hifigan}).
\end{itemize}

 This work explores neural synthesis options, which have shown a qualitative leap in quality and flexibility, although they require large amounts of data and computational power for training. The evolution of these systems is discussed in Section \ref{sec:estado_arte_tts}.

\subsection{NEURAL NETWORKS}
Neural networks are models parameterized by layers of artificial neurons that learn complex functions from data (\cite{goodfellow}).
In TTS and audio processing, diverse architectures are employed: convolutional neural networks (CNN) for time-frequency feature extraction; recurrent networks and Transformers (\cite{attention}) for sequence modeling; and \emph{attention} mechanisms in seq2seq tasks.

Neural networks make it possible to learn direct mappings (text $\rightarrow$ spectrogram) and generative models (vocoders, density models). Their flexibility explains the qualitative leap in TTS, but also the strong dependence on the quantity and quality of the training data.

\subsection{GENERATIVE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE}
Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) comprises techniques whose objective is to model the data distribution in order to generate new, plausible samples, whether images, text, or audio. The generation of these samples may or may not be conditioned on additional information (\emph{prompts}). The main contemporary paradigms are:


\begin{itemize}
\item Autoregressive models: they model the joint probability as a product of conditionals (e.g. PixelRNN for images (\cite{pixel_rnn}) or WaveNet for audio (\cite{wavenet})). They are conceptually simple and produce high quality, but can be slow at inference due to their sequential nature.
\item VAEs (Variational Autoencoders): latent models that optimize a variational bound on the likelihood. They offer good control of the latent space and stable training, but in general provide lower fidelity in raw samples (\cite{vaes}).
\item GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks): they pit two neural networks against each other, a generator vs. a discriminator competing as adversaries, where one network is in charge of generating samples and the adversarial network seeks to identify whether the generated samples are fake or not. This architecture generates high-fidelity samples quickly and efficiently (\cite{gans_ian}), although training can be unstable. This type of model has been successfully applied to vocoders and audio synthesis (e.g. HiFi-GAN by \cite{hifigan}).
\item Score-based / diffusion models: these include diffusion and score-matching models (DDPM, score-based models), which progressively add noise to real data and learn to reverse this process in order to sample (\cite{ddpm_diff}). They have currently shown competitive or superior results in sample quality and training stability in image and audio (e.g. DiffWave by \cite{diffWave}).
\end{itemize}

In audio/TTS, each paradigm has advantages: autoregressive models (WaveNet) achieved high naturalness; GANs (HiFi-GAN) offer fast inference and high fidelity; and diffusion models (DiffWave) combine quality with greater stability and conditional flexibility. The choice depends on trade-offs between quality, speed, and control.

\subsection{DIFFUSION MODELS}
Diffusion models are a class of probabilistic generators based on stochastic processes of noise addition and removal. Their modern formulation rests on two main ideas: (1) defining a forward process that corrupts the data by progressively adding Gaussian noise until almost pure noise is obtained; (2) learning the reverse process ---a conditional model that predicts denoising steps--- to transform noise into synthetic data.

The mathematical definition considers a data sample $x_{0}$, where the forward process adds noise over (T) steps:
\begin{equation}
    q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(x_{t};\sqrt{1-\beta_{t}},x_{t-1},\beta_{t}),
\end{equation}

with a variance schedule (${\beta_{t}}$). The reverse process is parameterized as:

\begin{equation}
    p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})=\mathcal{N}(x_{t-1};\mu_{\theta}(x_{t},t),\Sigma_{\theta}(x_{t},t)),
\end{equation}

and is trained by minimizing a variational bound on the evidence (ELBO) or an equivalent denoising loss. The work of \cite{ddpm_diff} formalizes this family (DDPM) and connects the denoising approximation with score-matching (\cite{score_based}).

Diffusion models provide competitive quality without adversarial training and allow conditional control (for example, conditioning on mel-spectrograms for vocoders). The main practical limitation is the sampling cost (multiple steps), although variants (DDIM, accelerated sampling, distillation) reduce the number of steps without substantial loss of quality.
