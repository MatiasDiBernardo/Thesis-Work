\subsection{DATASET COLLECTION}
In order to ensure the reproducibility and transparency of the experiments, the source code used to generate the figures and partial results presented in this thesis has been compiled. The material can be found in the following web repository: \url{https://github.com/MatiasDiBernardo/Audio-Analysis-Tesis}.

\subsubsection{\emph{In-the-wild} data}
To evaluate the processing pipelines on speech datasets, the Argentine Spanish corpus collected by the Intercambios Transorgánicos research group is employed. This corpus consists of 24 hours of recordings made under heterogeneous conditions, both in terms of audio quality and speaker diversity, and comes mostly from public sources on the internet. Owing to its variability and uncontrolled nature, this \emph{in-the-wild} dataset is well suited for validating audio preprocessing procedures. Hereafter, this collection will be referred to as the \emph{original} dataset (unprocessed version).

In order to capture dialectal differences relevant to Argentina, the selection of speakers follows the regional classification proposed by (\cite{Fontanella2004español}). Specifically, the corpus includes 32 speakers with a Buenos Aires (bonaerense) accent and 27 with a central accent (mainly corresponding to the province of Córdoba), for a total of 59 speakers. The future strategy is to broaden the dialectal coverage in order to incorporate all the representative varieties of the country; however, in this thesis the preprocessing pipeline is initially validated on these two representative variants.

\subsubsection{Professional data}
\label{sec:datos_profesionales}
To complement the \emph{in-the-wild} corpus and to have higher-quality material against which to contrast the objective analysis, professional datasets available from previous works are incorporated. Since there is no large public collection exclusively of Argentine Spanish, resources with closely related dialectal variants are also included where appropriate.

\begin{itemize}
\item ELRA Dataset (\cite{google-arg}): approximately 8 hours of audio with the Buenos Aires dialect, 44 speakers.
\item CML Dataset (\cite{cml-dataset}): an 8-hour subset of audio in Peninsular (Spain) Spanish, 15 speakers.
\item HaCASpa Dataset (\cite{dataset_hamburg}): approximately 10 hours of audio with the Mendoza dialect, 50 speakers.
\end{itemize}

The professional set totals 26 hours and 109 speakers, with an accent distribution that reflects regional diversity. This material is used as a reference to evaluate the acoustic quality and recording conditions attainable through the preprocessing pipeline applied to the \emph{in-the-wild} corpus.

\subsection{DEVELOPMENT OF THE PREPROCESSING PIPELINE}
\label{sec:pipeline-desarrollo}

The preprocessing pipeline designed in this Thesis is a modular and reproducible sequence of stages intended to transform \emph{in-the-wild} material into subsets usable for TTS training. The main stages are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Post-processing and metadata: Level normalization, metadata tagging (source, duration, dialect, capture conditions) and generation of the final subsets for evaluation and for feeding TTS models. It is important to highlight that this stage does not alter the audio signal, but rather organizes and prepares the data for subsequent analysis.
\item Voice Activity Detection (VAD): Removal of non-speech segments and generation of an initial segmentation into short fragments of continuous speech. The implementation uses Silero VAD (\cite{SileroVAD}), an algorithm with a series of hyperparameters that control the sensitivity of the detector and the minimum length of the segments. Tests were carried out to determine the optimal values of these parameters, and since no efficient trade-off was found, an adaptive hyperparameter optimization strategy was implemented, based on classifying the speech rate (slow/normal/fast) using Whisper timestamps and optimizing the VAD parameters per category.
\item Denoising / Speech enhancement: Optional stage of the pipeline aimed at mitigating noise and recording artifacts. Practical, CPU-efficient models are evaluated, in addition to the no-denoising option, considering the balance between perceptual improvement and preservation of vocal identity. The selected models are Demucs (\cite{demucs}) and DeepFilterNet (\cite{deepfilter}).
\item Non-intrusive quality filtering: Evaluation and filtering by means of non-intrusive perceptual quality models in order to accept or reject fragments according to a minimum score. Predictive MOS (Mean Opinion Score) models are used as a joint quality metric. The NISQA (\cite{nisqa}) and DNSMOS (\cite{dns_mos}) models are evaluated.
\item Transcription (STT): Generation of the automatic transcriptions needed to obtain the text-audio pairs required for training; at this stage, accuracy is prioritized at the expense of longer computation time, given the impact of transcription errors on the final quality of the corpus. The Whisper Large model is used for transcription (\cite{whisper}).
\end{enumerate}

The implementation emphasizes portability and low computational cost, so that groups with limited resources can reproduce the pipeline and compare variants of the algorithm without the need for high-performance hardware such as GPUs. \subsubsection{Different processing schemes}

To evaluate the impact of design decisions in the construction of preprocessing pipelines, the following variants are proposed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Denoising conditions: DeepFilterNet (DFN), Demucs, and \emph{no-denoising}. These alternatives represent intermediate points between efficiency, perceptual improvement and timbre preservation.
    \item Non-intrusive quality models: NISQA and DNSMOS, selected for their widespread use and their different sensitivity to noise conditions. These models are frequently used in the literature and exhibit different sensitivity to noise; here they are compared under different thresholds.
    \item Filtering thresholds: For NISQA, (3.0, 3.5, 3.8, 4.2) were evaluated, and for DNSMOS, (2.7, 3.0, 3.2, 3.4). The thresholds were chosen empirically by analyzing the predicted MOS level for the entire original dataset.
\end{itemize}

The threshold selection is based on an empirical analysis presented in Figure \ref{fig:original_mos}, where the MOS value produced by NISQA and DNSMOS is computed for all segments of the original dataset. It is interesting to note how DNSMOS exhibits a lower mean value and less variance. Consequently, in order to make a fair comparison, the thresholds were selected so as to ensure equitable filtering for both models.

Variations in the order of the pipeline stages were not considered due to a limitation of the denoising algorithms used. It would be interesting to evaluate the performance of the pipeline if the first process applied were denoising instead of VAD, but the Demucs implementation does not support long-duration audio (due to memory saturation issues). Likewise, the audio quality estimation algorithms work with short segments. Given these limitations, the established order is always maintained, in which VAD segments long-duration audio so that it can be processed by the subsequent stages without memory saturation issues.

The development of this pipeline follows the principles of open and reproducible code, so that other groups can replicate the experiments and compare various combinations of processes without the need for specialized hardware. The code follows a modular design principle so that it can be easily adapted and extended, allowing the different parts of the pipeline to be modified. The complete implementation is available in the GitHub repository \url{https://github.com/MatiasDiBernardo/Lowcost-ITW-curation}.

\subsection{DATASET EVALUATION}
\label{sec:metricas_description}

The evaluation is structured around four blocks of complementary metrics that capture quantity, signal quality, acoustic conditions and preservation of speaker characteristics. These metrics are combined into a composite metric that makes it possible to rank and select the best configuration according to the criteria defined in Section \ref{sec:metrica-conjunta}.

All metrics seek to quantify relative differences between the original dataset and the sub-datasets resulting from the different variants; in this way, the same methodology can be applied to characterize the behavior of the processing pipeline, regardless of the characteristics of the starting dataset.

\subsubsection{Corpus reduction}
Dataset size is measured as total duration (hours), and the \emph{data reduction} (RD) is quantified according to Equation \ref{eq:horas}, where the subscript P refers to the different alternatives of the processed dataset, and the subscript O refers to the number of hours of the original dataset.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:horas}
RD_P = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{HORAS}_P}{\mathrm{HORAS}_O}
\end{equation}

A smaller RD value is preferable (less data loss). This measure captures the basic trade-off between aggressive filtering conditions and the amount of usable material.

\subsubsection{Recording quality}
The evaluation of recording quality aims to quantify, in an objective and reproducible manner, the perceptual improvements and the signal fidelity obtained after applying the different stages of the pipeline (for example, denoising and filtering). To this end, complementary measures are employed: PESQ, SI-SDR and SNR. Initially, the possibility of also using STOI as a parameter to quantify intelligibility was considered, but empirically no significant differences were found in this value, so it was discarded.

PESQ (Perceptual Evaluation of Speech Quality) is used as an approximate indicator of the subjective quality of the signal by comparing the processed version with the original reference; SI-SDR (Scale-Invariant Signal-to-Distortion Ratio) measures signal fidelity in a manner robust to scale changes and makes it possible to evaluate how much of the original signal content is preserved after processing. The theoretical definition for computing these parameters requires an original version of the signal and a degraded version; since in this case the aim is to compare both datasets independently, the PyTorch Squim module (\cite{squim}) is used, which makes it possible to predict PESQ and SI-SDR values non-intrusively (blind estimation).

The original definitions of PESQ and SI-SDR are intrusive because they require a reference signal. In this thesis, their non-intrusive estimates obtained with SQUIM are used; hereafter, the abbreviated forms PESQ and SI-SDR refer to estimated PESQ and estimated SI-SDR, respectively. Likewise, MOS denotes a score automatically predicted by the corresponding model, unless a human listening test is explicitly indicated.

The SNR is estimated using WADA-SNR (\cite{wada}) to obtain a robust signal-to-noise ratio figure based on the waveform amplitude distribution. All these metrics are computed at the segment level and then summarized by the mean and standard deviation for each subset, so that it is possible to compare distributions before and after processing. Finally, the individual scores are integrated into the \emph{Signal Quality} (CS) block, where the influence of all these values is summed (Equation \ref{eq:cs}), taking into account that the PESQ, SI-SDR and SNR values are expected to increase (improvement) when the processing pipeline is applied. The subscripts follow the previous convention: P corresponds to the processed set and O to the original. This notation is kept consistent across all metrics.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cs}
CS_P =
\frac{\mathrm{PESQ}_O}{\mathrm{PESQ}_P}
\;+\; \frac{\mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR}_O}{\mathrm{SI\text{-}SDR}_P}
\;+\; \frac{\mathrm{SNR}_O}{\mathrm{SNR}_P}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Acoustic conditions}
The acoustic metrics aim to describe the room conditions and the presence of reverberation or late energy in the recordings, aspects that affect the usefulness of the audio for TTS training, especially the temporal alignment between text and mel-spectrogram. This work employs classical descriptors such as $T_{30}$ (approximate reverberation time) and clarity measures such as $C_{50}$ and $D_{50}$, which summarize the proportion of early energy relative to reverberant energy and make it possible to detect recordings with excessive reverberation or poor clarity.

Once again, it is necessary to compute these parameters blindly and without a reference of the original environmental conditions. Given these limitations, these metrics are estimated by means of a CNN model that was trained to compute acoustic parameters blindly and was validated for Argentine Spanish voices, so that the estimation is practical on \emph{in-the-wild} material without requiring room impulse responses (\cite{Maxi2}). The measurements are computed per segment and aggregated using statistics (mean and deviation) for each subset; the relative improvements are incorporated into the composite metric to evaluate whether a given combination of processes reduces reverberation and improves clarity with respect to the original set.

In this case, these two metrics are summed to account for the influence of the \emph{Acoustic Conditions} (CA) in Equation \ref{eq:ca}, where $T_{30}$ improves if it decreases, whereas $C_{50}$ improves if it increases. $D_{50}$ is discarded since the empirical results were very similar to the $C_{50}$ analysis, so redundant information would be added; consequently, it was decided to remove the contribution of this metric. The analysis of these variables makes it possible to discriminate processing schemes that improve the ``room condition'' of the corpus without excessively sacrificing other attributes.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ca}
CA_P =
\frac{T_{30,P}}{T_{30,O}}
\;+\; \frac{C_{50,O}}{C_{50,P}}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Speech differences}
The speech difference measures are intended to ensure that the preprocessing stages, in particular the denoising algorithms, do not unduly alter speaker identity or the prosodic variability of the corpus, which are critical aspects for synthesis with timbre and style preservation. The thesis considers two main indicators: the standard deviation of F0 (F0-STD) and the mean mel-cepstral distortion (MCD).

F0-STD captures the prosodic variability of the speakers; it is computed at the segment level using the PESTO estimator (\cite{PESTO}) for robust pitch extraction, and is compared between the original and processed versions to detect anomalous reductions in variability that would indicate a loss of naturalness or a bias in speaker selection. MCD is computed between the mel-cepstral coefficients of the original signal and of the processed signal (especially relevant for audio subjected to denoising) to quantify timbral changes (\cite{mcd_metric}); in the adopted formulation, MCD is expressed as a percentage or normalized increase relative to an acceptable reference value of 5 dB according to (\cite{survey2}), thereby penalizing schemes that significantly alter timbre.

These two components are combined into the \emph{Speech Differences} (DH) block, where any relative difference in F0-STD is penalized, and likewise for MCD with respect to the reference value (Equation \ref{eq:dh}). It should be noted that MCD is only computed for the variants with denoising, which somewhat favors the no-denoising alternatives that do not modify the speaker's characteristics.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dh}
DH_P =
\left|1 - \frac{\mathrm{F0std}_P}{\mathrm{F0std}_O}\right|
\;+\; \frac{MCD_P}{5}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Joint metric}
\label{sec:metrica-conjunta}
To rank configurations, a composite metric is proposed that sums the four previous blocks: dataset reduction (RD), signal quality (CS), acoustic conditions (CA) and speech differences (DH). This composite metric expresses mathematically the trade-offs assumed by the processing pipeline.

The composite metric is formulated as an optimization objective in Equation \ref{eq:metric_total}, where the aim is to minimize the negative contribution of each variable in order to identify the best variant P of the processing pipeline.

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:metric_total}
\min_{P \in \mathrm{Conf}}\;
\Big\{
RD_{P}
\;+\;
CS_{P}
\;+\;
CA_{P}
\;+\;
DH_{P}
\Big\}
\end{equation}

With this formulation, configurations that retain more hours, improve signal quality, improve (or maintain) acoustic conditions and preserve speaker characteristics will obtain lower (better) scores. An initial scheme is proposed in which all blocks have the same weight; choosing different weights makes it possible to prioritize, for example, vocal preservation over the number of hours if the goal is synthesis with high identity fidelity.

This metric is exploratory in scope and should be interpreted as a comparative descriptor of the evaluated configurations, not as a single physical measure of quality. The ratios that include variables expressed in dB are used to represent relative changes within the same corpus and to rank the configurations, without attributing a direct physical interpretation to them. However, the blocks combine different scales and magnitudes of variation; therefore, assigning them unit weight does not guarantee an equivalent influence on the ranking. Furthermore, the MCD term is only evaluated for the variants with enhancement and is taken as zero in the no-enhancement condition, which structurally favors the latter in the speech differences block. Equations \ref{eq:cs}, \ref{eq:ca}, \ref{eq:dh} and \ref{eq:metric_total} correspond to the implementation used for the results. A subsequent evaluation should normalize the blocks, justify weightings according to the intended use, and analyze the stability of the ranking under those alternatives.

\subsection{TRAINING OF THE DENSITY ESTIMATION MODEL}
Although the evaluation by means of traditional acoustic metrics (Section 4.3) offers a robust and interpretable approach for characterizing the datasets, this analysis has inherent limitations, since it restricts the evaluation to a discrete set of manually designed features. These metrics, while useful, may introduce biases by failing to capture all of the complex, high-dimensional dependencies present in the audio signal and in the problem itself.

To mitigate these limitations and provide a complementary perspective, an evaluation method based on representation learning is proposed, using a density estimation model. The fundamental objective of these models is to learn the intrinsic probability distribution $p_{data}(x)$ of a training dataset, obtaining an approximation of the underlying probability density function (PDF). The main advantage of this \textit{data-driven} approach lies in its unsupervised nature: the model extracts latent patterns and common characteristics of the reference distribution without relying on explicit labels or on the arbitrary selection of acoustic parameters, thereby reducing human intervention exclusively to the curation of the training set.

In the context of this thesis, the architecture and methodology proposed by (\cite{danilo2025_quality}) are adopted, who use generative models based on probabilistic diffusion to model the speech distribution. Their research shows that, by using high-fidelity audio as a reference to learn the base distribution, the model is able to estimate the quality of samples outside the training set by evaluating their likelihood. The reported results indicate that this approach exhibits a higher correlation with perceptual quality than traditional metrics such as PESQ or SNR, and a greater alignment with human subjective preference compared to predicted MOS.

The implementation proposed in this work uses the same principle of unsupervised learning, employing the professional dataset (described in Section 4.1.2) to train the model. In this way, a reference distribution is established that represents ``high-quality Spanish speech''. Once trained, the model makes it possible to compute the likelihood of the audio belonging to the different sub-datasets generated by the processing pipeline. Under the hypothesis that a higher likelihood indicates greater closeness to the reference distribution, this value is used as a comparative indicator between the evaluated configurations. On its own, it does not constitute a direct measure of perceptual quality, nor does it make it possible to determine which scheme best preserves the signal in all contexts.

\subsubsection{Handling of extreme values and aggregation}
The log-likelihood is computed for each evaluated audio crop. For the analysis, a post hoc filtered series is used: individual observations whose log-likelihood is greater than $4.0$ are excluded, and those equal to or below that threshold are retained. Inspection of the results showed extreme values in the upper tail that disproportionately influenced the means and the per-configuration distributions; therefore, the same cutoff is applied to all observations before computing means, distributions and associations. The filter acts on individual observations: it does not remove entire runs or configurations. As this is a post hoc decision, its results are interpreted as exploratory and subject to validation with more configurations and data.

\label{sec:model_config}

This resource was essential for handling the computational load required by high-fidelity diffusion models. \subsection{ZERO-SHOT TTS MODEL}
The exhaustive validation of a data curation pipeline would, in an ideal scenario, require the complete training of a synthesis model for each of the generated data subsets. However, due to computational constraints and the convergence times associated with training multiple neural models, this approach is unfeasible in the context of this research. In addition to these structural limitations, this method would have its own methodological limitations, since the inherent variability of the training process of synthesis models could introduce noise into the evaluation, making it difficult to attribute differences in synthesis quality exclusively to the characteristics of the dataset used.

Given this limitation, an alternative strategy based on voice cloning using \textit{zero-shot} TTS models is proposed. The underlying methodological hypothesis posits that the acoustic quality of the reference sample (the audio coming from the processing pipeline) could be associated with the perceptual quality of the synthesized voice. 200 samples were taken from each combination as a preliminary evaluation compatible with the available computational cost; this sample size does not guarantee statistical representativeness, nor does it by itself allow conclusive associations to be established. A larger number of samples and configurations would allow the variability of the synthesis to be better characterized.

To carry out this experiment, two models representative of the state of the art in \textit{zero-shot} synthesis were selected, grounded in diametrically opposed generative paradigms. First, the F5-TTS model (\cite{chen2024f5}) was used. This choice is based on three characteristics that are critical for the research:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Cloning capability with short references: The model has demonstrated robust performance using very short reference samples (under 30 seconds). This characteristic is indispensable, given that the strictest variants of the processing pipeline (e.g., high NISQA thresholds) may result in data subsets with limited availability of continuous segments.
    \item Architectural Coherence: F5-TTS is based on the \textit{Flow Matching} paradigm, a generalization of diffusion models. This aligns the architecture of the synthesis model with that of the density estimation model used for evaluation (Section 4.4), maintaining methodological consistency in the use of modern generative models.
    \item Availability and Reproducibility: Being an \textit{open source} model, it guarantees the reproducibility of the results and allows inspection of its internal workings.
\end{enumerate}

To contrast and enrich this analysis, a second synthesizer was incorporated: Qwen TTS (Hu et al., 2026). The selection of this second architecture, based on Large Language Models (LLMs), introduces a fundamental methodological counterweight:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Architectural Contrast (Discrete vs. Continuous Space): Unlike the continuous nature of F5-TTS, LLM-based models operate on discretized representations of audio generated by neural \textit{codecs} (acoustic \textit{tokens}). This makes it possible to evaluate how a model of autoregressive nature reacts to the same reference data.
    \item Resilience to Artifacts and Intrinsic Filtering: The discretization process of neural \textit{codecs} acts as an intrinsic normalization filter, giving the model greater computational resilience to residual noise or slight phase distortions. Its inclusion makes it possible to determine whether the destructive or beneficial impact of preprocessing affects all synthesizers uniformly, or whether it depends intrinsically on their underlying architecture.
\end{enumerate}

The experimental protocol consists of using representative audio from the different sub-datasets (selected both by objective metrics and by density estimation) as reference inputs for the F5-TTS system. To ensure that the evaluation is not biased by linguistic content, the sentences to be synthesized are taken from the \textit{"Los Sentidos"} corpus (\cite{lossentidos}). This text has been specifically selected because it is designed to cover the phonetic and allophonic diversity characteristic of Argentine Spanish.

Finally, to quantify the quality of the resulting syntheses and close the evaluation loop, a MOS (\textit{Mean Opinion Score}) predictor trained specifically to evaluate Spanish-language TTS systems (\cite{sosawelford25_interspeech}) is employed, which makes it possible to obtain an automated and consistent perceptual quality metric.

It is essential to emphasize that the results of this last experiment are preliminary and exploratory in nature. The prediction of subjective quality perception (TTS MOS) is subject to multiple factors inherent to the synthesizer models themselves (such as their prior training, their ability to adapt to the speaker and the complexity of the target text). Therefore, although this analysis reveals trends of great importance, future evaluations with standardized human listening test methodologies and cross-validation are required to establish categorical conclusions.
